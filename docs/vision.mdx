A bank history records money moving. It does not say how the movements fit together or what they mean over time. Bills arrive at different intervals. Purchases spread across accounts. One big month hides a small habit that turned into a lasting cost.

Ironcage gives one person a connected understanding of their own finances, with the life behind the records kept in view.

## Understanding change

The interesting questions are about change. Which costs went up? Which habits shifted? Did ordinary life get dearer, or did one unusual event skew the comparison?

Those questions need context. A mortgage changes the commitments. A trip explains a strange month. Moving everyday purchases to a credit card moves where payments appear without changing what was bought. The user supplies what the records cannot, and that explanation stays available for the next question.

## Decisions that reflect priorities

Saving matters, but the value of a purchase is personal. Spending supports relationships, interests, health, and comfort. Knowing what something costs gives the user room to decide what to change and what to protect. The point is informed choice, not a verdict that less is always better.

## Trust through evidence

A financial answer is worth something when its numbers agree with each other, its evidence is a click away, and its limits are stated. When records are incomplete or several explanations fit, that is part of the answer.

## Further out

Spend management comes first. The same foundation, with the same evidence and context, can later inform investments, trading, and other financial decisions. None of that has a stage yet.
